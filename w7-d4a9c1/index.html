POLS0007 · Week 7 seminar
Randomised experiments in practice
Does an AI tutor help students learn?
Two randomised trials, two different answers.

Five ideas to take away today

1
Random sampling lets us generalise. Random assignment makes the comparison fair.
2
Effects differ between people, so the effect in our sample (SATE) may not match the population (PATE).
3
If people drop out unevenly, the estimate is biased.
4
If people ignore their assignment, compare groups as assigned (ITT). The effect is diluted, never flipped.
5
With few randomised units, the estimate is imprecise: noisy, with no known direction.

Practice question 1 of 5
A UCL researcher randomly samples 300 first-year data science students from all UCL data science students, then randomly assigns 150 to use an AI tutor. Which is correct?
A
Sampling supports internal validity; assignment supports external validity.
✗ Reversed. Sampling is about who is in the study.
B
Both random steps support internal validity only.
✗ Sampling is about who is in the study, so it speaks to generalising.
C
Sampling supports external validity; assignment supports internal validity.
✓ Correct. Sampling → the 300 look like all UCL data science students. Assignment → groups alike, so a gap is due to the AI tutor.
D
Neither helps unless every sampled student takes part.
✗ Non-participation causes other problems, but each random step still does its own job.

Practice question 2 of 5
Kestin et al. found the AI tutor helped Harvard physics students who volunteered for the study. A UCL lecturer says the result will hold at UCL. What is the best reason to doubt this?
A
The AI tutor may help some types of students more, and these volunteers may not resemble UCL students.
✓ Correct. Impact variation plus an unrepresentative sample: the effect for these students (SATE) may differ from UCL’s (PATE).
B
Only 194 students were eligible, so the study is too small to say anything.
✗ A small sample affects precision, not whether the result transfers. They also found a clear effect.
C
Students were randomly assigned, which makes results hard to generalise.
✗ Random assignment supports internal validity. It does not harm generalising.
D
The post-test was short, so the groups cannot be compared.
✗ Test length affects what is measured, but the question is about moving the finding to a new group.

Practice question 3 of 5
Students do either an AI-tutor lesson at home or a class lesson. The at-home lesson is harder, so weaker students in the AI tutor group are more likely to skip the post-test. The estimated effect of the AI tutor will probably be:
A
Biased downwards (understated): weaker students make the AI group look worse.
✗ The weaker students are the ones missing, so the AI group left looks better.
B
Unbiased, because students were randomly assigned.
✗ Random assignment protects the groups at the start, not who drops out afterwards.
C
Impossible to sign, because we do not know who skipped.
✗ We are told who is likely to skip, so we can reason about direction.
D
Biased upwards (overstated): the AI group left in the analysis is stronger.
✓ Correct. Weaker students leave only the AI group, so its average rises and the effect looks too big.

Non-compliance and take-up

10 students randomly offered the AI tutor
What they actually did
● used it (3)   ○ never used it (7)
Take-up = 3 / 10 = 30%
Non-compliance
Assigned ≠ treated. We still see their outcome.
Attrition
We lose their outcome.
Practice question 4 of 5
Which of these is an example of non-compliance?
A
Some students in both groups skip the final exam.
✗ Skipping the exam means we lose the outcome. That is attrition.
B
Some instructors assigned to the Virtual Study Assistant (VSA) never switch it on.
✓ Correct. They were assigned the treatment but never received it. We still see their students’ outcomes.
C
The study took place at only one university.
✗ Where the study took place is about external validity.
D
Only 30 instructors were randomised.
✗ Few randomised units is about precision, not ignoring assignment.

Intention-to-treat (ITT)

Offered an AI tutor (100)
● 30 used it   ● 70 did not
Average: 55
Not offered (100)
 
Average: 50
ITT = 55 − 50 = 5
Everyone offered vs everyone not offered
Users only: 65 − 50 = 15 ✗
Users chose themselves: biased
Practice question 5 of 5
500 students are offered a revision app and 500 are not. Of the 500 offered, 200 download it. Average exam scores: all 500 offered = 62; the 200 who downloaded = 70; not offered = 58. What is the ITT effect?
A
12 (users 70 minus not offered 58)
✗ That is users only. They chose to download, so they are not comparable.
B
8 (users 70 minus offered 62)
✗ That compares part of a group with the whole group it belongs to.
C
4 (offered 62 minus not offered 58)
✓ Correct. ITT compares everyone offered (62) with everyone not offered (58).
D
0 (most offered students did not use it)
✗ Low take-up shrinks the ITT but does not make it zero.

Bias vs dilution vs imprecision

Bias
Pushed one way by something systematic.
0true effect
e.g. differential attrition. Too big or too small.
Dilution
Real effect, but many never got the treatment, so it shrinks towards zero.
0true effect
e.g. low take-up. Too small, never flips sign.
Imprecision
Few units, so the estimate is noisy.
0true effect
e.g. 30 instructors. No known direction.

Five things to remember

1
Sampling → external validity. Assignment → internal validity.
2
Sample effect (SATE) is not automatically the population effect (PATE).
3
Uneven drop-out → bias.
4
Ignoring assignment → dilution. Compare as assigned (ITT).
5
Few units → imprecise.
