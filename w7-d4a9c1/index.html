Five ideas to take away today

1
Random sampling and random assignment do different jobs
Sampling → can we generalise (external validity). Assignment → is the comparison fair (internal validity).
2
Ask three questions of any experiment
Who was randomised? Who is still in the analysis (attrition)? Who actually got the treatment (take-up)?
3
The average effect in the sample is not automatically the average effect for everyone
Effects differ between people, so who is in the study matters.
4
Name the problem and its direction
Bias pushes the estimate one way. Dilution shrinks it towards zero. Imprecision makes it noisy, with no known direction.
5
A good design fixes the problems you spotted
Randomise at the right level, compare with a realistic control, measure the same outcome for everyone at a sensible time.

Practice question 1 of 5
A UCL researcher randomly picks 300 first-year science students from all UCL science students. She then randomly gives 150 of them an AI tutor. Which statement is correct?
A
Random picking supports internal validity; random giving of the tutor supports external validity.
✗ Reversed. Picking students is a sampling step, which is about who is in the study.
B
Both random steps support internal validity only.
✗ Picking students is about who is in the study, so it speaks to generalising (external validity).
C
Random picking supports external validity; random giving of the tutor supports internal validity.
✓ Correct. Picking → sample looks like all UCL science students. Giving the tutor at random → the two groups are alike, so a difference can be put down to the tutor.
D
Neither helps unless every selected student takes part.
✗ Non-participation causes other problems, but the two random steps still do their separate jobs.

Practice question 2 of 5
Kestin et al. found that the AI tutor helped Harvard physics students who volunteered. A UCL lecturer says the result will hold at UCL. What is the best reason to doubt this?
A
The tutor may help some types of students more than others, and these volunteers may not resemble UCL students.
✓ Correct. This is impact variation plus an unrepresentative sample: the effect for these students (SATE) may differ from the effect at UCL (PATE).
B
Only 194 students were eligible, so the study is too small to say anything.
✗ A small sample affects precision, not whether the result transfers. It also found a clear effect.
C
Students were randomly assigned, which makes results hard to generalise.
✗ Random assignment supports internal validity. It does not harm generalising, and it is not the issue here.
D
The post-test was a few questions long, so the groups cannot be compared.
✗ Test length affects what is measured, but the question is about moving the finding to a different group.

Practice question 3 of 5
In a study, students do either an AI lesson at home or a class lesson. The at-home lesson is harder, and weaker students in the AI group are more likely to skip the post-test. The estimated effect of the AI tutor will probably be:
A
Biased downwards (understated), because weaker students make the AI group look worse.
✗ The weaker students are the ones missing from the AI group, so the AI group left is better, not worse.
B
Unbiased, because students were randomly assigned.
✗ Random assignment protects the groups at the start. It does not protect against who drops out afterwards.
C
Impossible to sign, because we do not know who skipped.
✗ We are told who is more likely to skip, so we can reason about the direction.
D
Biased upwards (overstated), because the AI group left in the analysis is stronger on average.
✓ Correct. Weaker students leave only the AI group, so its average rises and the AI effect looks too big.

Non-compliance and take-up

10 students randomly offered the tutor
Only 3 actually use it
Take-up = 3 out of 10 = 30%
Non-compliance: people do not do what they were assigned (assigned but did not use it, or in the control group but used it anyway).
Attrition: we lose their outcome.
Non-compliance: we still see their outcome. They just did not take the treatment.
Practice question 4 of 5
Which of these is an example of non-compliance?
A
Some students in both groups skip the final exam.
✗ Skipping the exam means we lose the outcome. That is attrition.
B
Some instructors assigned to the Virtual Study Assistant never switch it on.
✓ Correct. They were assigned the treatment but never received it. We still see their students’ outcomes.
C
The study took place at only one university.
✗ Where the study took place is about external validity.
D
Only 30 instructors were randomised.
✗ Few randomised units is about precision, not about people ignoring their assignment.

Intention-to-treat (ITT)

100 students randomly offered a free tutor
100 students not offered
Only 30 of the offered students use it
Average exam score:
offered group (all 100) = 55   not offered = 50
Intention-to-treat (ITT) = 55 − 50 = 5
Compare groups as they were randomised, whatever people did. Keeps the fair comparison.
Per-protocol (compare the 30 users, avg 65, with the 50 not offered) = 15
Looks bigger, but users chose to use it. Motivated students pick themselves, so this is biased.
ITT is what Liu et al. report. With low take-up it is small, because most of the group was never treated.
Practice question 5 of 5
500 students are offered a revision app and 500 are not. Of the 500 offered, 200 download it. Average exam scores: offered group (all 500) = 62; the 200 who downloaded = 70; not offered = 58. What is the ITT effect?
A
12 (users 70 minus not offered 58)
✗ That is a per-protocol comparison. Users chose to download, so they are not comparable with the not-offered group.
B
8 (users 70 minus offered 62)
✗ That compares part of a group with the whole group it belongs to. It is not a comparison between the randomised groups.
C
4 (offered 62 minus not offered 58)
✓ Correct. ITT compares the groups as randomised: everyone offered (62) against everyone not offered (58).
D
0 (most offered students did not use it)
✗ Low take-up shrinks the ITT, but it does not make it zero. We can read it straight from the two group averages.

Bias vs dilution vs imprecision

Bias
The estimate is pushed one way by something systematic.
0true effect
e.g. differential attrition. Too big or too small. Do not shrink with a bigger sample.
Dilution
The effect is real, but many people never got the treatment, so the average shrinks towards zero.
0true effect
e.g. low take-up. Too small. Cannot flip the sign.
Imprecision
Few units, so the estimate is noisy. It could land above or below the truth.
0true effect
e.g. 30 instructors. No known direction.

Q1: Kestin. My answer

1
External validity: too big
The sample is Harvard physics volunteers. If the tutor helps motivated, high-achieving students more, the effect for other students is smaller, so this estimate is too big for them.
2
Internal validity: too big
Post-test data came from 142 of the AI group but 174 of the class group (194 eligible). If weaker students dropped out of the AI group, the AI group left looks stronger, so the effect is overstated.
3
Not my main picks
A small sample is about precision (and they still found a clear effect). Short post-tests matter, but they are a weaker point than who is in the study and who is missing.

Q1: Liu. My answer

1
Low take-up: too small
Only about 15% of treated students used the VSA. Most of the “treated” group was never treated, so the ITT estimate is diluted towards zero. It does not bias the comparison, because assignment was still random.
2
Few units: imprecise, direction unknown
Instructors were randomised, so there are only 30 independent units. The estimate is noisy and could be off in either direction.
3
Not my main picks
Control students using ChatGPT shrinks the gap too, and dropping instructors needs flagging. But take-up and the number of units are the clearest and easiest to defend.

Table 3: three things to look for

1
Which way?
The estimate = treated average minus control average. Negative = treated did worse.
2
Is it just noise?
The standard error (SE) in brackets says how much the estimate would wobble if we reran the lottery. An estimate more than about 2 SEs from zero is unlikely to be chance. Stars show this.
3
How big?
Divide by the control SD (standard deviation: how spread out students are). That gives the effect in SDs, so we can compare with Kestin’s +0.63 SD.

Q2: why do the findings differ?

Kestin and Liu tested AI tutors. Kestin found a large benefit. Liu found a small, borderline negative effect.
Kestin: +0.63 SD
Liu: about −0.27 SD
Q2: why might the findings differ? Two questions to ask of every explanation:
1. Could it explain a smaller effect?
2. Could it explain a negative one?

Q2, factor 1: take-up (100 students)

Liu: about 15 of every 100 treated students used the tutor. The other 85 never did.
Suppose the tutor changes users’ scores by some amount, and changes non-users’ scores by 0.
Average change for all 100 = 0.15 × (change for users). The effect is diluted: shrunk towards zero.

Q2, factor 1: what take-up can and cannot do

If Kestin’s +0.63 SD applied to the 15% who used it:
0.15 × 0.63 ≈ +0.09 SD. Smaller, but still positive.
Liu’s estimate is −2.68 points. For users alone to produce that, they would need to lose 2.68 ÷ 0.15 ≈ 18 points (about −1.8 SD).
So low take-up explains why Liu’s effect is small. It cannot explain why it is negative. Dilution shrinks towards zero. It does not flip the sign.
A negative result needs something else: the tutor hurting learning, effects on non-users (for example, instructors changing how they teach), or chance. The grade effect is only borderline (about 1.7 SEs).

Q2: four possible factors

1. Treatment vs control
Kestin: AI at home vs a strong active-learning class. Liu: VSA available vs nothing extra, with other AI tools open to all.
2. Take-up
Kestin: students did the lesson. Liu: ~15% used the VSA.
3. The tutor itself
Kestin: tutor built to guide learning. Liu: default gave direct answers (73.8% of questions asked for information or solutions). Tutor mode: 2 instructors.
4. The outcome
Kestin: short post-test straight after. Liu: final grades and engagement over a semester.

Q3: three boxes

Design your own study. Each of the three boxes should fix a problem we found in Kestin or Liu.
1
Unit of randomisation
Who or what is randomised?
2
Treatment and control
What does each group get?
3
Outcome and timing
What is measured, and when?
For each box: several options, what goes wrong with each, then the one I would pick.

Q3, box 1: why not randomise students?

Randomise students inside one course
Treated and control students sit in the same class and share notes, prompts and answers.
⇄
Randomise whole courses
Everyone in a course gets the same condition, so there is nobody to share with.
Contamination (spillover): control students benefit from the treatment. It makes the gap too small. But whole courses means fewer units, which means more imprecision.

Q3, box 1: unit of randomisation

A
Let students choose whether to use the tutor
Self-selection: motivated students opt in, so the groups are not comparable. Not random at all.
B
Randomise individual students inside courses
Fair comparison and many units, but contamination: students share the tutor’s answers.
C
Randomise whole courses
Avoids spillover. Fewer units, so noisier, and course differences (subject, level) can swamp the effect.
D
Randomise whole courses within blocks (department and year)
Avoids spillover and compares like with like. Best balance. Blocking also improves precision.
★ I would go for this

Q3, box 2: treatment and control

A
Treatment: tutor. Control: nothing, with AI banned
Cannot enforce a ban, and it tests the tutor against a world that does not exist.
B
Treatment: tutor. Control: nothing extra
Fine, but unclear what “nothing” means when students can use ChatGPT.
C
Treatment: general AI chatbot. Control: business as usual
Tests the chatbot, but not a tutor built to guide learning.
D
Treatment: guided tutor inside Moodle. Control: business as usual, including ChatGPT
Answers the real policy question: does a guided tutor beat what students already do? Easy to scale up.
★ I would go for this

Q3, box 3: outcome and timing

A
Student survey: how much do you feel you learned?
Quick, but measures belief, not learning, and treated students may over-rate it.
B
Moodle activity (page views, active days)
Measures engagement, not learning. Careful: Liu found these fell.
C
Each instructor’s own exam
Exams differ in difficulty, so groups are not comparable.
D
One common exam: AI-free, invigilated, marked blind. Plus a follow-up next term
Same test for all, marked without knowing the group, and the follow-up checks whether learning lasts.
★ I would go for this

Q3: one full design

Unit: randomise whole courses, blocked by department and year, to avoid contamination and compare like with like.
Treatment / control: a guided tutor in Moodle vs business as usual (including ChatGPT), so we learn what a real rollout would add.
Outcome / timing: a common AI-free, invigilated, blind-marked exam at the end of term, plus a follow-up next term to see if it lasts.
Each choice answers a problem we found: contamination, an unrealistic control, an outcome that cannot be compared or that fades.

Five things to remember

1
Sampling → external validity. Assignment → internal validity.
2
Ask: who was randomised, who is left, who got the treatment?
3
Sample effect (SATE) is not automatically the population effect (PATE).
4
Name the problem and its direction: bias, dilution, imprecision.
5
Fix each problem in your design: unit, treatment/control, outcome and timing.
